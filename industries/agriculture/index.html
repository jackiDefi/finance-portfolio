Agriculture: enormous revenue, razor-thin margin, the spread is everything.

How a global agri-processor actually earns, why the margin is measured in pennies per tonne, where the working capital goes, and what a defensible 5-year model looks like — applied to ADM.

Section 1 · Primer

How the business works

A global agri-processor is a volume machine running on a wafer-thin spread. It originates crops from farmers, transports and stores them across a global logistics network, processes them (crushing oilseeds, milling, refining, fermenting) into oils, meals, sweeteners, starches and ingredients, and trades the flows in between. Revenue is gigantic because it is essentially the value of the commodities flowing through the system — but the company keeps only a sliver of it. Net margins of 2-4% are normal. This is the defining feature: a business where revenue tells you almost nothing and the spread tells you everything.

Because the commodities themselves are pass-through, the processor is not really betting on the price of corn or soybeans — it hedges that away. What it earns is the processing margin (the crush spread between the cost of the bean and the value of the oil and meal it becomes) and the merchandising margin (buying in one place and time, selling in another). Both are spreads, both are volatile, and both depend on supply-demand dislocations, weather, trade flows and freight — not on the absolute commodity level.

Revenue & margin drivers

The useful identity is not revenue but contribution:

Operating profit ≈ volume (tonnes) × margin per tonne

Volume is the throughput of the asset base — crush capacity utilisation, elevator and port throughput, ingredient demand. Margin per tonne is the spread, and it is where the cycle lives. When the crush spread is wide (strong meal/oil demand, ample cheap beans), processing prints money; when it compresses, the same fixed asset base earns very little. The skill of the trading desk — reading global flows, positioning ahead of dislocations — is a genuine but opaque edge, much like a bank’s.

Cost structure

The dominant line, by far, is the cost of the commodities bought to process and trade — often 90%+ of revenue, almost entirely pass-through and hedged. What is left:

  • Processing & conversion costs — energy, labour, plant operating cost. Energy is a major swing factor for crushing and refining.
  • Logistics & freight — rail, barge, ocean. A real competitive moat for whoever owns the network; a margin drain when freight spikes.
  • SG&A — relatively modest against the revenue base.
  • D&A — low as a % of revenue (because revenue is so large) but meaningful against the asset base.

The thinness of the residual margin means a small move in the spread or in energy/freight costs is a large move in profit. Operating leverage cuts both ways and fast.

Working capital — the defining balance-sheet feature

This is where agri-trading is unusual. The business carries enormous inventory (crops in store and in transit) and receivables, and these swing violently with commodity prices. When prices rise, working capital absorbs billions of cash even if volumes are flat; when they fall, it releases cash. A large slice of the debt on the balance sheet is readily-marketable-inventory (RMI) financing — debt secured against hedged, liquid inventory, which analysts often strip out to get to “adjusted” net debt. Modelling the working-capital cycle is not optional here; it is the cash-flow story.

KPIs analysts watch

  • Segment operating profit — Ag Services & Oilseeds, Carbohydrate Solutions, Nutrition; where the money actually is
  • Crush margin / spread per tonne — the cycle in one number
  • Volume & capacity utilisation — throughput of the asset base
  • Adjusted ROIC vs WACC — the through-cycle value test
  • Adjusted net debt / EBITDA — leverage ex-RMI financing; ~2x typical
  • Cash conversion — how much of EBITDA survives the working-capital swing

Valuation

EV/EBITDA on normalised mid-cycle EBITDA is the working tool — processors trade at 6-9x, with the higher-margin, more stable ingredient/Nutrition businesses deserving a premium to the commodity-trading core. The single biggest error is anchoring on a peak or trough year: 2021-2022 were exceptional crush years, 2024 was weak, and a multiple applied to either extreme misleads. DCF is the cross-check, with WACCs of 7-9% and a terminal value built on a normalised, not spot, margin. P/E and dividend yield matter for the steadier names.

Common analyst pitfalls

  1. Looking at revenue instead of margin. Revenue moves with commodity prices and tells you almost nothing; segment operating profit and margin per tonne are the signal.
  2. Capitalising a peak or trough margin. The crush spread is mean-reverting; valuing the business on an exceptional year — high or low — is the classic mistake.
  3. Mis-reading the debt. Treating RMI-financing debt as if it were structural leverage overstates the risk; ignoring the working-capital cash swing overstates free cash flow.

Section 3 · Case study

Archer-Daniels-Midland (NYSE: ADM)

Applying the model to one of the “ABCD” global agri-traders — neutral analytical stance, no recommendation.

ADM is a clean test case for the thin-margin processor because it spans the full chain — origination and merchandising (Ag Services & Oilseeds), processing (Carbohydrate Solutions), and the higher-margin ingredients business (Nutrition) — and because FY2024 was a visibly weak year for it: crush margins normalised hard from the 2022-2023 highs, and the company also worked through an accounting issue in its Nutrition segment. That makes it a useful illustration of the central valuation trap: do you capitalise the trough, or a normalised mid-cycle margin?

The model sits modestly above where ADM trades, and the reason is the whole lesson of the sector. The DCF is fed a normalised 5.5% EBITDA margin; the market in 2024 was staring at a trough crush spread and a Nutrition write-down, and priced the stock closer to the bad year than to mid-cycle. If you instead capitalise the trough margin, the model collapses toward the market; if you capitalise the 2022 peak, it balloons well above. The entire valuation comes down to one judgement — what is the through-cycle margin per tonne — and the thin-margin maths means a half-point of EBITDA margin moves the equity value by billions.

Where the case study could break

Bull case — what re-rates it: crush spreads recover toward mid-cycle as biofuel (renewable diesel) demand pulls on vegetable oil; Nutrition stabilises and re-rates as a steadier, higher-margin ingredients business; cost programmes lift the through-cycle margin toward 6.5%. On a normalised 6.5% margin the model would sit well above $70 a share.

Bear case — what compresses it: a prolonged glut keeps crush spreads depressed; energy and freight costs stay elevated; the Nutrition franchise underperforms and the trading desk has a poor year. A sustained 4.5% margin pulls the model down toward — or below — the current market price, and the working-capital swing in a falling-price environment masks the cash generation.

Why the model and the tape disagree: they are pricing different margins. The market, mid-2024, anchored on a weak year; the model anchors on a normalised spread. Whether the gap is opportunity or a value trap depends entirely on whether you believe the crush margin mean-reverts up — which is a view on global supply, weather, and biofuel policy, not on ADM’s assets.

What I’d do differently in a deeper write-up: model the three segments separately, each on its own margin and capacity path, and value Nutrition on a higher multiple than the trading core; build an explicit crush-margin / spread-per-tonne deck rather than a single EBITDA margin; carry a full working-capital schedule driven by commodity price and DIO/DSO so the cash swing is visible; and split structural debt from RMI financing in the leverage view.
