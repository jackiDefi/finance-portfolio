Oil & Gas: a price-taker that prints cash — until it doesn’t.

How an integrated major actually earns, what the commodity cycle does to the P&L, where the capital goes, and what a defensible 5-year model looks like — applied to Shell plc.

Section 1 · Primer

How the business works

An integrated oil major is three businesses bolted together: upstream (find and lift hydrocarbons), downstream (refine, trade, and market fuels and chemicals), and increasingly low-carbon / power. The defining feature of all of them is that the company is a price-taker on a globally-traded commodity it does not control. Volume is an engineering decision made years in advance; price is set by the marginal barrel on any given day. That single fact — cost is committed, revenue is volatile — explains almost everything about how the sector behaves.

The cycle is the master variable. When Brent is high, the upstream segment throws off cash far in excess of its cost base, balance sheets de-lever, dividends rise and buybacks accelerate. When Brent collapses, the same fixed cost base turns the operating leverage against the company, free cash flow evaporates, and capex is the first thing cut. A model that does not stress the price deck is not a model of an oil company.

Revenue drivers

For the upstream business, the identity is close to:

Upstream revenue ≈ production (boe/d) × 365 × realised price ($/boe)

Two levers, very different character. Production is slow-moving and capital-determined — new barrels come from multi-year projects, and the base field declines 3-8% a year without reinvestment, so a chunk of capex just stands still. Realised price is the cycle — a blend of oil (Brent / WTI), gas (Henry Hub / TTF / JKM), and LNG, each on its own curve, discounted from the benchmark by quality and location. Downstream adds refining margins (the crack spread) and trading, which is a genuine edge for the majors and a black box to outsiders.

Cost structure

The big lines, roughly in cash-cost order:

  • Lifting / operating cost — the cash cost to produce a barrel once the field exists. $5-15/boe for a good major; the number that decides who survives a downturn.
  • Royalties & production taxes — the host government’s take, often the largest single claim on a barrel.
  • Purchases — crude and product bought for the refining and trading book; the largest line on the integrated P&L, low-margin flow-through.
  • DD&A — depletion of capitalised wells and facilities on a unit-of-production basis; non-cash but real.
  • Exploration & write-offs — dry holes and impairments; lumpy, cycle-correlated.

Upstream cash margins per barrel are enormous at high prices and negative at low ones. Downstream is the opposite — thin, steady, and a partial hedge, because cheap crude is a cost input for the refiner. Integration is the point: the segments do not peak together.

Capex cycle

Oil & gas is among the most capital-intensive sectors in existence. There is maintenance capex (offset the base decline, keep facilities safe) and growth capex (new projects, multi-year, with long payback). Total intensity has run 6-12% of revenue for a disciplined major, but the number that matters is capex versus operating cash flow — the post-2014 and post-2020 doctrine is capital discipline, returning the surplus to shareholders rather than chasing volume. The market now rewards restraint, not growth.

Working capital & the balance sheet

Inventory (crude and product) and receivables swing hard with price, so working capital can consume or release billions in a single quarter purely on the commodity move. Net debt is managed to a target gearing band; the dividend is treated as quasi-sacred, so debt absorbs the cycle. Decommissioning provisions — the legal obligation to plug wells and remove platforms — are a large, long-dated liability that DCFs routinely under-weight.

KPIs analysts watch

  • Production (mmboe/d) and reserve replacement ratio — is the company replacing what it pumps?
  • Reserve life (1P / 2P, years) — runway at current production
  • Unit lifting cost ($/boe) — downturn survivability
  • Free cash flow breakeven ($/bbl Brent) — the single most-watched number
  • Cash flow from operations & FCF — the dividend / buyback engine
  • Gearing (net debt / total capital) — cycle headroom
  • Shareholder distribution (% of CFFO) — the capital-discipline scorecard

Valuation

EV/EBITDA and EV/DACF (debt-adjusted cash flow) are the working tools — majors trade at a low 3-6x forward EV/EBITDA, structurally de-rated by the energy-transition overhang. P/CF and free-cash-flow yield are the cleaner frames for income-oriented holders. DCF is the cross-check, with WACCs of 8-11% reflecting commodity and stranded-asset risk, and a price deck that should be explicit and stressed. NAV / sum-of-the-parts (value each asset on its own reserves and cost curve) is the M&A frame. The dividend yield — often 4-6% — sets a floor.

Common analyst pitfalls

  1. Modelling a flat price deck. A single Brent assumption hides the entire risk profile. The sensitivity to price is the analysis, not a footnote to it.
  2. Ignoring the base decline. Treating maintenance capex as optional, or assuming flat production for free, overstates FCF — barrels run downhill without reinvestment.
  3. Under-weighting decommissioning and transition capex. The end-of-life cleanup bill and the cost of the low-carbon pivot are real, long-dated cash that flatters near-term FCF if left out.

Section 3 · Case study

Shell plc (LSE: SHEL)

Applying the model to the most balanced of the European integrated majors — neutral analytical stance, no recommendation.

Shell is the right test case because it is the cleanest expression of the integrated model: a top-tier upstream portfolio, the world’s largest LNG trading business, a deep downstream and chemicals footprint, and a low-carbon arm being managed for returns rather than scale. Post-2023 it has pursued an explicit capital-discipline doctrine — capped capex, sold non-core assets, and routed the surplus into a steady dividend and a large, sustained buyback. That makes its reported numbers a good calibration anchor for a price-taker run for cash rather than growth.

This is the opposite of the result a well-covered telecom incumbent gives. With an entirely defensible mid-cycle set of drivers — mid-single-digit revenue, 23% EBITDA margin, disciplined 7.5% capex — the DCF says Shell is worth materially more than it trades for: roughly $56 a share against a market near $34, a 5.7x implied multiple against a traded 3.5x. The model is not wrong and the market is not wrong. The gap is the analysis. A standard DCF cannot see the energy transition; it extends today’s cash flows into perpetuity at a positive terminal growth rate. The market, pricing in demand-plateau and stranded-asset risk, refuses to. The entire valuation debate in this sector lives in that wedge — which is why a flat-deck DCF is a starting point, not an answer.

Where the case study could break

Bull case — what re-rates it: a structurally tighter oil market lifts Brent into the $90-100 band; LNG demand outruns supply through the late-2020s and Shell’s trading book captures the spread; the buyback keeps shrinking the share count into a rising FCF base. EBITDA margin pushes toward 27-28%, FCF compounds, and the multiple closes some of the gap to the US majors at 5-6x.

Bear case — what compresses it: demand peaks earlier than expected and Brent settles in the $60s; refining margins normalise from post-2022 highs; transition capex rises without near-term returns; a higher WACC on stranded-asset fear. EBITDA margin slips below 20%, the dividend competes with the buyback for a shrinking cash pool, and the multiple stays stuck near 3x. Equity value 25-35% lower.

Why the DCF and the tape disagree: Shell generates cash well in excess of its committed capex at mid-cycle prices, gearing is inside its target band, and the dividend is covered with room to fund buybacks — so a cash-flow model that trusts those flows into perpetuity values the equity richly. The market does not trust them into perpetuity. It applies a transition discount: a higher effective discount rate, a sub-GDP or negative terminal growth, and a refusal to capitalise a demand-plateau business at utility multiples. Both views are internally consistent; the difference is entirely a view on long-run hydrocarbon demand. That is the honest output of this exercise — not a price target, but a measured size of the bet the market is making.

What I’d do differently in a deeper write-up: split upstream / downstream / LNG-trading / chemicals / low-carbon into separate segments, each on its own price deck, margin and capex profile; model production with an explicit base-decline and project-by-project ramp rather than a single growth rate; build a stated Brent / Henry Hub / TTF deck and run the FCF breakeven; and carry decommissioning provisions as an explicit long-dated cash schedule.
