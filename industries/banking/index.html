Banking: a leveraged spread business that lives and dies on risk.

How a bank actually earns, why the balance sheet is the business, what moves the margin, and what a defensible 5-year model looks like — applied to HSBC.

Section 1 · Primer

How the business works

A bank is not an industrial company with a balance sheet attached — the balance sheet is the product. It borrows money (deposits, wholesale funding) at one rate and lends it (loans, securities) at a higher one, keeping the spread. Then it does this many times over its own capital: a bank funds maybe twelve to fifteen dollars of assets with one dollar of equity. That leverage is the entire point and the entire danger. A small move in the value of the assets — a wave of defaults — is a large move in the equity. Banking is a spread business wrapped around a risk-management problem, and the risk management is what separates the survivors from the headlines.

Because of that leverage, banks are not valued like other companies. There is no enterprise value — debt is raw material, not financing — so EV/EBITDA is meaningless. Banks are valued on equity: price-to-book (or price-to-tangible-book), return on tangible equity, and the dividend they can sustainably pay. The regulator, not the CFO, sets the ceiling on how much risk the equity can support.

Revenue drivers

Income splits into two streams. The first is net interest income (NII):

NII ≈ average interest-earning assets × net interest margin (NIM)

Earning assets is the volume lever — loans, plus the securities and central-bank balances the bank holds. NIM is the price lever — the blended yield on assets minus the blended cost of funding, typically 1.2-2.0% for a large diversified bank, higher for a retail-heavy or emerging-market one. NIM is exquisitely sensitive to the rate cycle: rising rates usually widen it (assets reprice faster than deposits), falling rates compress it. The second stream is non-interest income — fees, commissions, cards, wealth management, trading. Fee income is prized because it earns a return without consuming the balance sheet or much capital.

Cost structure

Two cost lines dominate, and they behave completely differently:

  • Operating expenses — staff, technology, branches, compliance. Watched through the cost-to-income ratio (opex / total revenue); 45-60% is the normal band, and the long-run direction is down as banks digitise. Quasi-fixed.
  • Loan-loss provisions (cost of risk) — the expected-credit-loss charge for loans going bad. This is the swing factor. In benign years it is 20-40 basis points of loans; in a recession it can spike to 150-300bps and erase a year of profit. The whole game is pricing this risk correctly up front.

Pre-provision operating profit is the clean read on the franchise; provisions are where the cycle — and management’s underwriting discipline — show up.

Capital & the regulatory frame

This is the line that has no analogue in any other sector. A bank must hold capital against its risk-weighted assets (RWA) — assets scaled by how risky the regulator deems them. The key ratio is CET1 (Common Equity Tier 1) / RWA, with regulatory minimums plus buffers putting the working floor around 11-14%. CET1 is the binding constraint on everything: how much the bank can lend, how much it can pay in dividends, how much it can buy back. A bank generating capital above its target can return the surplus; one below it must rebuild, cutting distributions. Liquidity has its own ratios (LCR, NSFR). You cannot model a bank without modelling its capital.

KPIs analysts watch

  • Return on tangible equity (ROTE) — the headline; mid-teens is excellent, <8% destroys value
  • Net interest margin (NIM) — the spread, and its rate sensitivity
  • Cost-to-income ratio — efficiency; 45-60% band
  • Cost of risk (bps) — the credit cycle in one number
  • CET1 ratio — capital headroom for growth and distributions
  • Loan / deposit ratio — funding self-sufficiency
  • NPL ratio & coverage — asset-quality stress and provisioning adequacy

Valuation

The master relationship is the justified price-to-tangible-book identity: P/TBV = (ROTE − g) / (CoE − g). A bank earning a ROTE above its cost of equity is worth more than book; one earning below trades at a discount. That single equation explains most of why bank multiples differ. The dividend-discount and residual-income models are the formal cross-checks; CoE typically sits at 9-12%. Banks rarely trade above 1.5-2.0x TBV (only the very highest-ROTE franchises) and frequently below 1.0x when ROTE lags CoE. Dividend yield matters because well-capitalised banks return a lot of capital.

Common analyst pitfalls

  1. Valuing a bank on EV/EBITDA or a DCF of operating cash flow. Leverage is the business; equity-based methods (P/TBV, ROTE, DDM) are the only correct frame.
  2. Straight-lining the cost of risk. Provisions are cyclical and the single biggest swing factor — a model with flat provisions is a model that has never seen a recession.
  3. Ignoring the capital constraint. Growth, dividends and buybacks are all gated by CET1. A forecast that grows the loan book without funding the RWA is internally inconsistent.

Section 3 · Case study

HSBC Holdings plc (LSE / HKEX: HSBA / 0005)

Applying the model to a globally-systemic, Asia-anchored universal bank — neutral analytical stance, no recommendation.

HSBC is a clean test case for the equity-based frame because it is large, diversified, well-capitalised, and run explicitly to a ROTE target. Post-2023 it has reshaped around its Asian core — exiting Canada and Argentina, simplifying the structure, and committing to a mid-teens ROTE with heavy capital return through dividends and buybacks. That makes its disclosed metrics a good anchor for a NIM / cost-of-risk / capital model.

The result is the textbook bank story in one line. With a normalized ROTE of ~13.5% sitting comfortably above a 10.5% cost of equity, the justified-P/TBV identity says the equity is worth more than its tangible book — about 1.4x — because the bank is compounding capital at a return above what shareholders require. The market trades it closer to 1.0-1.1x, applying a haircut for the things the formula cannot see: geopolitical concentration in Hong Kong and mainland China, the through-cycle reality that cost of risk will not stay at 20bps, and the discount global investors attach to a bank whose fate is tied to one region’s property and policy cycle. The gap between ~1.4x justified and ~1.05x traded is the China-risk discount, quantified.

Where the case study could break

Bull case — what re-rates it: rates stay higher-for-longer and NIM holds near 1.7%; Asian wealth and transaction-banking fees compound at high-single digits; cost discipline pushes the cost-to-income ratio below 45%; buybacks keep shrinking the share count against a rising ROTE. Sustainable ROTE pushes toward 15-16%, justified P/TBV toward 1.6x, and the China discount narrows.

Bear case — what compresses it: rate cuts compress NIM toward 1.3%; a mainland China property and credit downturn lifts cost of risk to 60-80bps and dents fee income; a higher cost of equity on geopolitical stress. ROTE slips toward 9-10%, the justified multiple falls below 1.0x, and the stock trades at a discount to tangible book. Equity value 25-35% lower.

Why the model and the tape disagree: the justified-P/TBV identity is brutally sensitive to two inputs — the sustainable ROTE and the cost of equity — and the entire valuation debate for HSBC is whether the through-cycle ROTE really clears the cost of capital once a normalised cost of risk and a fair geopolitical risk premium are applied. The model, fed benign-cycle inputs, says yes and prices a premium. The market, pricing the tail, says “show me,” and holds it near book. Neither is wrong; the disagreement is entirely about the right cost of risk and the right CoE.

What I’d do differently in a deeper write-up: build the balance sheet from loans / deposits / securities separately with their own yields and funding costs rather than a single NIM; carry an explicit RWA and CET1 schedule so growth, dividends and buybacks are capital-constrained; model cost of risk with an explicit through-cycle path and a stress scenario; and split the franchise by region (Asia / UK / rest) given how concentrated the value and the risk both are.
