01 — Connectivity · Live

Telecom & IT: the rent collected on connectivity.

How operators actually make money, what moves the margin, where the capex goes, and what a defensible 5-year model looks like — applied to Saudi Telecom (STC).

Section 1 · Primer

How the business works

Telecom is the rent collected on connectivity. Customers — consumer, business, government — pay monthly (postpaid) or up front (prepaid) for access to a network the operator finances, builds, maintains, and increasingly resells software services through. Every month a portion of the base churns; every month the operator spends to acquire and retain the next cohort. The economics are subscription-business economics, dressed in capital-intensive plumbing.

The reported P&L mixes two distinct businesses: service revenue (connectivity, billed recurring) and equipment revenue (handset sales, one-off, low or negative gross margin, used to subsidise customer acquisition). Service revenue is what compounds; equipment revenue is a flow-through. Sell-side notes that talk about “revenue growth” without isolating service revenue tend to miss the signal.

Revenue drivers

For the consumer business, the identity is:

Service revenue ≈ subscribers × ARPU × (1 − churn rate)

Three levers, three different reflexes. Subscribers is the volume game — market share in a relatively fixed addressable market (often 110-130% mobile penetration in mature markets because of dual-SIM). ARPU is monetisation per subscriber, where mix shifts (prepaid→postpaid, voice→data, mobile→convergent bundles, B2C→B2B) live. Churn is the leakage rate — postpaid churn of 0.5-1.5% per month is healthy in mature markets; prepaid churn of 3-5% is normal. A one-point reduction in monthly churn moves the steady-state base by a multiple.

For enterprise and wholesale, the drivers shift to connections × ARPU + ICT services revenue (cloud, cybersecurity, managed services, IoT). This is where the modern integrated telco — STC, du, Telkom Indonesia — increasingly competes. The economics are different: lower asset intensity, higher margin volatility, longer sales cycles.

Cost structure

The big lines, roughly in P&L order:

  • Cost of goods / interconnect — handset COGS, interconnect to other operators, content. Variable.
  • Network opex — site lease, tower rent, energy, transmission, maintenance. Mostly fixed once built.
  • Employee costs — technical, retail, back office. Quasi-fixed; hard to flex down quickly.
  • Customer acquisition — handset subsidies, dealer commissions, churn-prevention spend. Lumpy.
  • D&A — typically 15-20% of revenue, reflecting fibre, towers, core, and spectrum amortisation.

EBITDA margins post-IFRS 16 sit in the 35-45% band for mature operators; pre-IFRS 16 numbers are usually 5-7 points lower because operating leases hit opex rather than D&A and interest. Always check which the operator reports.

Capex cycle

Telecom is one of the more capital-intensive sectors in services. Maintenance capex is high-single-digit % of revenue. Total intensity has run 14-18% in steady state and pushed to 18-25% during the 4G build (2013-2018), the 5G rollout (2020-2025), and large fibre programmes. Spectrum auctions are episodic and large — multi-billion cash outflows in the year of payment, then amortised over 15-30 years. DCFs that treat spectrum as opex understate the front-loaded cash hit.

Working capital

Postpaid creates DSO of 60-90 days. Prepaid is cash-positive — money in before service is rendered. Enterprise contracts carry 90-180-day DSOs on managed services. Inventory matters when handsets are subsidised. WC is rarely the swing factor in valuation but can mask or amplify FCF in any given year.

KPIs analysts watch

  • Service revenue growth (organic, FX-neutral) — 0-3% mature, 3-6% growth markets
  • EBITDA margin (post-leases) — 35-45% target band
  • Capex intensity — 14-18% steady state, higher during build cycles
  • FCF conversion (FCF / EBITDA) — 25-40% healthy
  • Net debt / EBITDA — 1.5-3.0x typical; covenants usually 3.5-4.0x
  • Postpaid share of base — proxy for ARPU stickiness
  • ARPU trajectory — leading indicator on mix shift and pricing power

Valuation

EV/EBITDA is the working tool. Mature integrated telcos trade at 5-7x forward EV/EBITDA; growth-market and digital-services-heavy names get 6-9x. DCF is the cross-check, with WACCs at 7-10% in emerging markets, 5-7% in EU mature. Dividend yield is often the cleanest frame — mature telcos are run as bond proxies, with 5-7% yields and progressive payouts. EV per subscriber is used in M&A.

Common analyst pitfalls

  1. Mixing service and equipment revenue in growth analysis. Handset cycles distort year-on-year comps; only service revenue compounds.
  2. Ignoring the IFRS 16 step-change in EBITDA (effective 2019). Reported EBITDA jumped 5-10 points with no underlying change. Comparisons across the 2018-2019 boundary need normalisation.
  3. Treating spectrum payments as opex or smoothing them. Spectrum is real, large, lumpy cash. Models that spread it evenly overstate FCF in auction years.

Section 3 · Case study

Saudi Telecom Company (Tadawul: 7010)

Applying the model to the integrated incumbent in the GCC’s largest economy — neutral analytical stance, no recommendation.

STC is the right test case because the company looks like a generation-ahead version of the operator I work for: a state-anchored incumbent that has moved from pure mobile/fixed into an integrated telco-plus-digital posture, with growing enterprise and cloud revenues sitting alongside a still-significant consumer base. The KSA consumer market is also one of the few in the region where ARPU is still rising, helped by Vision 2030 spending, expat inflows, and a postpaid mix that’s structurally above regional peers.

The result is deliberately uneventful. With a defensible set of drivers, the model converges to roughly where the market already is — which is what you’d expect for a large, well-covered incumbent in a developed-information market. The interesting work is in where the central case is wrong.

Where the case study could break

Bull case — what gets the stock to a premium: Vision 2030 enterprise capex accelerates B2B / cloud revenue (STC Solutions) at double-digit rates while consumer mobile holds. EBITDA margin re-rates to 38-40% as cloud scales and Egypt integration synergies land. Capex intensity normalises to 15% post-2027 as 5G coverage matures. In that world, FCF expands meaningfully and the multiple is more like 8.5-9x.

Bear case — what compresses it: ARPU compression in mobile from prepaid competition; Egypt asset underperforms; spectrum and fibre obligations keep capex sticky at 18-20% for longer than expected; KSA rate environment lifts WACC by 100bps. EBITDA margin slips to 34%, FCF conversion weakens, multiple drifts to 6.5x. Equity value 25-30% lower.

Why the central case sits where it does: STC’s ROCE has historically been well above WACC, leverage is manageable, and the dividend policy (progressive, with special distributions) underpins a yield floor. The integrated digital story is real but not yet at the scale where it changes the multiple in a binary way. A market that priced STC like a 4-5% yielding utility, with mild B2B-led growth, would land roughly where it trades today. The model agrees.

What I’d do differently in a deeper write-up: split the consumer and enterprise segments and model B2B/cloud on its own growth and margin profile (currently mixed in service revenue); explicitly separate spectrum cash from maintenance capex; build a working-capital schedule from DSO/DPO trends rather than the simplifying 2%-of-incremental-revenue rule used here.
